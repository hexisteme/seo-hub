The Decision Bias Audit

A checklist to run before any decision that's expensive, hard to reverse, or made under pressure — anchoring on a number, buying because everyone else is, or continuing a project because you've already sunk time into it.

What this is: a self-audit. Every item below is something to watch for *in your own thinking* before you commit to a decision.

What this is not: a manual for reading, exploiting, or pressuring other people. If you're looking for ways to use these patterns *on* someone else — in a negotiation, a sale, a relationship — that's outside what this document covers, on purpose. Recognizing a bias in yourself and engineering it into someone else are different skills, and only the first one is in here.

A note on the science: the biases below are ones with a long, repeatedly-replicated research record (largely from behavioral economics and judgment-and-decision-making research, going back to Kahneman and Tversky's work in the 1970s–80s). Some famous "psychology facts" from the same era — the Stanford Prison Experiment, ego depletion, power posing — have since failed to replicate in follow-up studies and are deliberately left out of this list. If a claim can't hold up to a repeat test, it doesn't belong in an audit tool.

The audit

Before a decision that's expensive, hard to reverse, or time-pressured, scan this table. You don't need to find all ten — even catching one changes the decision.

BiasThe tell (how it shows up)Counter-move
AnchoringThe first number you heard (asking price, initial offer, "recommended" tier) is still doing the work in your head, even after you've seen other options.Before comparing options, write down what you'd pay/choose with no anchor in sight. Compare your independent number to the anchored one.
Sunk cost fallacyYou're justifying continuing with "but I've already put so much in" rather than "this is still the best use of what's left."Ask: "If I were starting today, with none of this already spent, would I still choose this path?" If no, the sunk cost is the only thing holding you.
Confirmation biasYou notice yourself searching for reasons you're right rather than reasons you might be wrong; sources that agree with you feel more "credible."Deliberately find and read the strongest version of the opposing case before deciding — not the weakest, easiest-to-dismiss version.
Planning fallacyYour time or cost estimate matches how it usually goes for people in general, and you're confident this time is the exception.Ask someone who has done this before (not you) how long it actually took them, and add a buffer based on their answer, not yours.
Availability heuristicYou're weighting a risk or outcome heavily because a vivid example comes to mind easily — not because it's actually common.Ask: "Is this vivid because it's frequent, or just because it's memorable?" Look for a base rate instead of a memory.
Loss aversionYou're more moved by what you might lose from choosing X than by what you'd gain from choosing X — even when the amounts are equal.Reframe the decision in both directions: "What do I gain by switching" and "what do I lose by staying," and check if they're actually symmetric.
OverconfidenceYour stated certainty ("I'm sure this will work") isn't backed by a track record of being right about similar calls before.Write your prediction down with a confidence percentage before the outcome is known. Check it later. Most people's calibration is worse than their confidence.
Status quo biasThe default option feels safer for no reason you can articulate other than it's what's already happening.Ask: "If the status quo weren't already in place, would I actively choose it today?"
Social proof / bandwagon"Everyone's doing this" is carrying more weight in your reasoning than the specifics of your own situation.Ask what you'd decide if you couldn't see what anyone else was doing — using only information about your own case.
Halo effectYou're extending trust in one area (a person's charisma, a brand's design quality) into an unrelated area (their financial judgment, their product's safety).Evaluate the specific claim on its own evidence, separate from your overall impression of the source.

How to use it

  • Keep this as a Notion checklist and duplicate it per decision — don't try to hold all ten in your head at once.
  • The "tell" column is the important one to read slowly. Most people can recite bias names; almost no one notices the tell in the moment it's happening to them.
  • If two or more tells show up on the same decision, slow down before committing — that's a signal worth treating seriously, not a coincidence to wave off.

Decision Bias & Cognitive Biases — Prompt Pack

Ten prompts for Claude or ChatGPT, built to pair with the Decision Bias Audit. Every prompt below is framed around auditing *your own* decision — none of them ask the model to help persuade, pressure, or read another person. If you want to adapt one of these for use on someone else, that's a different tool than this pack.

1. Pre-Decision Bias Check

What it's for: Runs a decision you're about to make through the full bias audit before you commit.

I'm about to make this decision: [describe the decision, what's at stake, and your
current leaning]

Here is a list of cognitive biases: anchoring, sunk cost fallacy, confirmation bias,
planning fallacy, availability heuristic, loss aversion, overconfidence, status quo
bias, social proof/bandwagon, halo effect.

For each one, ask me one specific yes/no or short-answer question that would reveal
whether that bias might be influencing this particular decision (not a generic
definition — a question specific to what I described). Wait for my answers before
telling me which biases seem most likely to be in play.

Swap: your decision description.

2. Pre-Mortem Generator

What it's for: A structured way to imagine failure before it happens, as a counter-move to overconfidence and planning fallacy.

I'm planning to: [describe your plan]

Run a pre-mortem: assume it's [a realistic future date] and this has failed. Generate 5
distinct, plausible reasons it failed — not vague ones like "bad luck," but specific
mechanisms tied to my actual plan. For each reason, suggest one concrete change I could
make now to reduce that specific risk.

Swap: your plan and a realistic timeframe.

3. Steelman the Opposing View

What it's for: A direct counter-move to confirmation bias — forces the strongest version of the case against your current position.

My current position on [topic/decision] is: [state your position and your main reasons
for holding it]

Write the strongest possible case against my position — not a strawman, the actual best
argument a smart, informed person who disagrees with me would make. Then tell me
honestly: does this change how confident I should be, and why or why not?

Swap: your position and reasoning.

4. Sunk Cost Reframe

What it's for: Helps separate what you've already invested from what's actually the best path forward.

Here's what I've already invested in this: [time, money, effort — be specific]
Here's the decision I'm facing now: [continue, pivot, or stop — and why you're
hesitating]

Help me answer this question only: if I were starting today with none of what I've
already invested, would I still choose to continue? Don't factor my past investment into
your answer — that's the sunk cost, and it's deliberately excluded from this
calculation.

Swap: your actual investment and decision.

5. Base Rate Reality Check

What it's for: A counter-move to the availability heuristic — checking whether a vivid risk is actually common.

I'm worried about this risk: [describe the specific outcome you're afraid of, and why
it feels likely — often because you heard a vivid example]

Help me think through: is there a general base rate for how often this actually happens
to people in situations like mine, separate from the vivid example that's on my mind?
If you don't have a reliable general answer, say so rather than guessing a number — I
just want you to help me separate "memorable" from "common."

Swap: the risk and why it feels top-of-mind.

6. Planning Fallacy Time Buffer

What it's for: Adjusts your own time estimate using outside-view reasoning instead of your gut feeling.

I estimate this task/project will take: [your estimate]
Here's what it involves: [describe the actual work]

Ask me: has something similar taken me or someone I know longer than expected before?
Based on my answer, help me set a more realistic estimate using an outside-view approach
(how long do similar projects generally take) rather than my inside view (how it feels
like it should go).

Swap: your estimate and task description.

7. Loss/Gain Symmetry Check

What it's for: A counter-move to loss aversion — checking whether you're weighting a potential loss more heavily than an equivalent gain.

I'm deciding between [option A] and [option B]. Here's what I'm afraid of losing if I
choose one over the other: [describe it]

Reframe this decision twice: once purely in terms of what I gain by choosing each
option, and once purely in terms of what I lose by choosing each option. Help me check
whether my current leaning is being driven more by the loss-framing than the
gain-framing, even though the underlying amounts are the same.

Swap: your two options and what you fear losing.

8. Calibration Log Starter

What it's for: A counter-move to overconfidence — starting a simple prediction tracking habit.

Help me set up a lightweight decision journal. For this decision: [describe it and your
prediction of the outcome], write me a one-line entry with: the decision, my prediction,
my confidence level (as a percentage), and a date to check back and see if I was right.
Keep the format short enough that I'll actually keep doing this for future decisions too.

Swap: your decision and prediction.

9. Status Quo Stress Test

What it's for: Checks whether you're staying with a default option out of habit rather than genuine preference.

I'm currently doing/using/staying with: [the status quo option]
The alternative I'm considering: [the alternative]

Ask me: if I were choosing fresh today, with no history of already doing the status quo
option, which would I pick? Help me separate "this is genuinely still the best choice"
from "this is just what I'm already doing."

Swap: your current default and the alternative.

10. Post-Decision Review

What it's for: A closing-the-loop prompt — reviewing a past decision honestly after the outcome is known, to build your own track record for prompt 8's calibration log.

Here's a decision I made earlier: [the decision, my prediction at the time, my
confidence level if I recorded one]
Here's what actually happened: [the real outcome]

Help me review this honestly: was I right, wrong, or partially right? If I was
overconfident or underconfident, which specific bias from this list (anchoring, sunk
cost, confirmation bias, planning fallacy, availability heuristic, loss aversion,
overconfidence, status quo bias, social proof, halo effect) most likely contributed, and
what's one concrete adjustment for next time?

Swap: your past decision, prediction, and actual outcome.
