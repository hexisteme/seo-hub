Mathematical Thinking & Fallacy Audit Sheet

For analytical readers who want to catch five specific reasoning traps before they distort a real decision — plain-language explanations, sourced numbers, no proofs required.

🧭 Why These Five?

These five results were picked because each one is peer-reviewed or documented in a canonical academic source, and each one exposes a *different* way careful people misjudge probability — not the same mistake five times. Every number below is sourced; nothing here is rounded up from a guess.

What this is: five well-established statistical and probability results, explained in plain language, with one self-check question each — something to run before a decision that hinges on "how likely is this, really."

What this is not:

  • Not a betting system. None of the five below are framed as a way to win at casino games, sports betting, or picking stocks. The math describes closed, known-rule scenarios (a game show with three doors, a fixed set of numbered drawers, a leading digit's distribution) — it doesn't transfer to games built so the house has the edge.
  • Not a verdict on any live social or political debate. Simpson's Paradox especially gets pulled into current arguments. This sheet sticks to the mechanism and to one specific, peer-reviewed historical dataset that demonstrates it — it doesn't extend that case into a general claim about any other dataset or debate.
  • Not a wall of notation. Where a formula is genuinely the fastest way to say something, it appears once, in plain terms, immediately translated into a percentage you can use.

The Five Paradoxes

1. The Monty Hall Problem

The setup: You pick one of three doors. Behind one is a prize; the other two are empty. The host — who knows what's behind every door — opens one of the two doors you didn't pick, always revealing an empty one, and asks if you want to switch to the last remaining door.

1. What intuition says: Two doors are left, so it's now 50/50 — switching can't matter.

2. What's actually true, and why: Switching wins 2 times out of 3; staying wins 1 time out of 3. Your first pick had a 1-in-3 chance of being right *at the moment you made it*, and nothing about that changes just because a door was opened afterward — the host isn't picking randomly, he's guaranteed to reveal an empty door among the two you didn't choose. Group the other two doors together: as a group, they had a 2-in-3 chance of hiding the prize. Once the host removes one guaranteed-empty option from that group, the group's entire 2-in-3 probability collapses onto the single door left in it — the one you'd switch to.

3. Where this shows up in real decisions: Any time someone who knows more than you deliberately removes one option and asks if you want to change your pick — a hiring manager narrowing finalists after new information, a doctor ruling out a diagnosis after a test result, a counterparty taking one item off the table mid-negotiation. The "even odds now" instinct assumes the removal was random — check whether it actually was before you treat the odds as reset.

4. The check-yourself question: *Was this option removed at random, or because someone who knows more than I do chose to remove it? If it wasn't random, my original odds haven't reset to even — they've shifted onto whatever's left.*

Source: Selvin, S. (1975). "A problem in probability" [Letter to the editor]. *The American Statistician*, 29(1), 67.

2. Simpson's Paradox

The setup: In 1973, UC Berkeley's graduate admissions showed men were admitted at a noticeably higher rate than women overall. (One specific, peer-reviewed dataset — not a claim about any other debate on this topic.)

1. What intuition says: If the combined, aggregate numbers show one group admitted at a lower rate, that gap must reflect bias running through the process that produced it.

2. What's actually true, and why: Overall, about 44% of the 8,442 male applicants were admitted versus about 35% of the 4,321 female applicants — a real aggregate gap. But broken out department by department, there was no general pattern of bias against women; several departments leaned the other way. The reversal happened because women disproportionately applied to more competitive departments with lower admit rates for *everyone*, while men applied more to less competitive ones. The aggregate number was tracking which departments people applied to, not how any given department treated men versus women.

3. Where this shows up in real decisions: Comparing an aggregate success rate across two groups — two sales teams, two hospital units, two program cohorts, two years of a process — when the underlying mix (deal size, patient risk, starting difficulty) differs between them. The aggregate number can move, or even reverse, purely because the mix shifted, with nothing changing at the level where decisions are actually made. This doesn't mean aggregate gaps are never meaningful — it means the aggregate alone can't tell you whether the gap comes from within-group treatment or from a shift in composition. That requires breaking the data out.

4. The check-yourself question: *Do the two things I'm comparing have the same underlying mix of subgroups — or could a shift in that mix explain the number by itself, before I look for any other cause?*

Source: Bickel, P.J., Hammel, E.A., & O'Connell, J.W. (1975). "Sex Bias in Graduate Admissions: Data from Berkeley." *Science*, 187(4175), 398–404.

3. The 100 Prisoners Problem

The setup: 100 numbered prisoners face 100 drawers, each holding one number in a random arrangement. Each prisoner may open 50 drawers looking for their own number, one prisoner at a time, with no communication once the process starts (though they can agree on a strategy beforehand). Every single prisoner must find their own number, or none of them go free.

1. What intuition says: Each prisoner opening drawers looks like a roughly 50/50 shot at finding their own number, so with 100 people all needing to succeed independently, the group's odds should be catastrophically small — and no strategy should change that, because you can't out-think 100 independent coin flips.

2. What's actually true, and why: If every prisoner opens drawers independently at random, the intuition is exactly right: joint success sits at roughly (1/2)^100 — an astronomically small number, well below one in a trillion. But there is a strategy that changes the picture entirely: each prisoner first opens the drawer matching their own number, then opens whichever drawer's number they just found, repeating up to 50 times (a "follow the chain" strategy). This lifts the group's joint survival probability to just over 30% — commonly cited as about 31%. It works because the drawers aren't 100 independent coin flips; they're a single random arrangement that decomposes into cycles, and every prisoner following the chain finds their own number if and only if they're on a cycle no longer than 50. All 100 succeed together exactly when the *longest cycle in the whole arrangement* is 50 or shorter — one shared, correlated event, not a hundred separate ones.

3. Where this shows up in real decisions: Multi-step processes where several people each have to succeed for a joint outcome — an approval chain, a relay of handoffs, a checklist where every item matters. Treating each step as an independent risk and multiplying probabilities can badly understate what's actually achievable if the steps can be *coordinated* instead of left separate.

4. The check-yourself question: *Am I estimating this multi-step outcome by treating each step as independent, when the steps might actually share a structure I could coordinate — and would the real odds change if I did?*

Source: Gál, A., & Miltersen, P.B. (2003). "The Cell Probe Complexity of Succinct Data Structures." *Proceedings of the 30th International Colloquium on Automata, Languages and Programming (ICALP 2003)*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 2719, Springer.

4. Benford's Law

The setup: Take a large collection of numbers that arise naturally and span several orders of magnitude — city populations, river lengths, physical constants, financial transaction amounts. Look only at each number's leading (first) digit.

1. What intuition says: With nine possible leading digits (1 through 9), each should show up roughly equally often — about 11% each.

2. What's actually true, and why: The leading digit isn't uniform. Digit 1 shows up about 30% of the time, and each higher digit shows up less often, down to about 5% for digit 9 — a logarithmic pattern rather than a flat one (the rule is P(digit d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d), which gives roughly 30% for 1 and roughly 5% for 9). The pattern was first noted by astronomer Simon Newcomb in 1881, who observed that the early pages of logarithm tables — covering numbers starting with 1 — were far more worn than the later pages. Physicist Frank Benford independently rediscovered and rigorously documented the same pattern across dozens of unrelated datasets in 1938.

3. Where this shows up in real decisions: Forensic accounting and auditing use deviation from the Benford pattern as a first-pass screening flag. A large dataset of self-reported or entered numbers (expense reports, invoice totals, transaction records) whose leading-digit distribution looks suspiciously flat, or otherwise doesn't match the expected pattern, doesn't prove fraud or error on its own — but it's a recognized signal auditors use to decide where to look more closely.

4. The check-yourself question: *If I'm looking at a large set of self-reported or manually entered numbers, does the leading-digit pattern look organic — or does it look like someone picked round, evenly spread numbers instead of numbers that occurred naturally?*

Sources: Newcomb, S. (1881). "Note on the Frequency of Use of the Different Digits in Natural Numbers." *American Journal of Mathematics*, 4(1), 39–40. Benford, F. (1938). "The Law of Anomalous Numbers." *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society*, 78(4), 551–572. Applied use in fraud screening: Nigrini, M.J. (2012). *Benford's Law: Applications for Forensic Accounting, Auditing, and Fraud Detection*. John Wiley & Sons.

5. The Birthday Paradox

The setup: 23 people are in a room. Assume 365 equally likely birthdays (leap years set aside) and no relationship between any two people's birthdays.

1. What intuition says: With 365 days in a year and only 23 people, the odds that two of them share a birthday should be small — something like 23 ÷ 365, or about 6%.

2. What's actually true, and why: The probability that at least two people share a birthday is about 50.7% — better than even odds. The intuition above compares one person against the group; what actually matters is every *pair* of people in the room. With 23 people there are 23 × 22 ÷ 2 = 253 distinct pairs, and each pair is a separate chance at a match. Working the exact calculation (multiplying the odds that each successive person's birthday avoids every birthday already claimed, all the way through all 23 people) gives roughly a 49.3% chance of *no* match at all — so the chance of at least one match is 1 minus that, about 50.7%.

3. Where this shows up in real decisions: Any time you're impressed by a "coincidence" — two people you know sharing an unusual trait, two entries in a large dataset matching, two independent reports lining up. The question that actually matters isn't "how likely was this one match," it's "how many possible matches could there have been." The same shape of question — count the pairs, not the individuals — is also the starting point for how cryptographers reason about collision risk in large systems.

4. The check-yourself question: *How many comparisons were actually possible here — and once I count all of them instead of just the one I happened to notice, is this "coincidence" actually likely rather than remarkable?*

Source: Feller, W. (1968). *An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications*, Vol. 1, 3rd ed. Wiley, pp. 31–32.

How to Use It

  • Keep this table somewhere you'll actually reopen before a consequential decision — a Notion page, a sticky note, whatever you'll see in the moment.
  • You don't need to identify the paradox by name in real time. The "ask yourself" column is the operative part — the name is just how you'll remember which question applies.
  • None of these five questions tell you what to decide. Each one just removes one specific, well-documented kind of probability error from the reasoning before you decide.
