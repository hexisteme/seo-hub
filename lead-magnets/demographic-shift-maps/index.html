2030 Global Demographic & Risk Shift Matrix

For anyone tracking where labor supply, capital, and geopolitical leverage are headed next — without reading a 300-page UN report to find it.

🧭 Why This Matrix Exists

Population age structure is one of the slowest-moving, most-predictable forces in economics — the people who will be 45 in 2040 are already alive today. Yet most coverage of it arrives as a single headline ("China's population is shrinking") with no sense of scale, timing, or what region is absorbing the shift.

This matrix answers one question per region and per country: is its working-age population (15–64) still expanding, or has it already peaked? That single fact is the input behind labor migration flows, pension-system strain, manufacturing relocation, and which economies still have a "demographic dividend" window left to use.

Scope note: this is a data summary of population structure, not a forecast of unrest, a migration-policy argument, or a claim that any country or people group is a "problem." The numbers below describe labor supply, not intent.

📌 The One Number Worth Remembering

Between 2025 and 2050, Sub-Saharan Africa's working-age population is projected to add more than 620 million people — over three-quarters of the entire net increase across all emerging market and developing economies combined.

⚠️ What the Shift Creates: A Regional Risk Summary

The matrix above answers "still gaining workers or already peaked?" This table answers the second half of that question: what constraint does each region's position on that curve create? These are structural constraints implied by the age-structure data above, not forecasts of unrest or a claim that any region or country is a "problem" — a rising dependency ratio is a fiscal-planning fact, not a crisis prediction.

🌐 The Global Backdrop

  • World population: 8.2 billion (2024), projected to peak around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, then decline gradually to roughly 10.2 billion by 2100.
  • Global fertility rate: 2.3 births per woman (2024), down from 3.3 in 1990.
  • Global life expectancy: 73.3 years (2024), up 8.4 years since 1995; projected to reach roughly 77.4 years by 2054.
  • In about 100 countries and areas, the working-age population (20–64) will keep growing through 2054 — the "demographic dividend" window. In 63 countries (28% of the 2024 world population), total population size had already peaked before 2024.
  • By the late 2070s, the global population aged 65+ (projected at 2.2 billion) is expected to outnumber the population under 18 for the first time.
  • India passed China to become the world's most populous country in 2023.

*Source (India–China ranking): UN DESA, Population Division, Policy Brief No. 153, "India overtakes China as the world's most populous country" (April 2023). Source (remaining figures above): UN DESA, Population Division, World Population Prospects 2024: Summary of Results — Ten Key Messages (July 2024).*

What This Doesn't Cover

Numbers on projected GDP impact, pension-fund shortfalls, and country-specific migration policy were left out — the sourcing behind the figures circulating for those (IMF, national pension agencies, various think tanks) didn't hold up to a direct-quote check, and this document doesn't carry a number it couldn't trace to a named report and year. If you need those, go to the primary report — for pensions, the relevant national or EU Ageing Report; for growth impact, the IMF's country-specific Article IV consultations.
