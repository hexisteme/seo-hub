Stripe pricing explained: what Stripe keeps from a $10 sale, a $100 sale, a $1,000 sale and a $10,000 sale, by payment method

Fact-checked and signed by Seonrye Kim, who checks each figure against the provider's own published pricing page. · Updated 2026-09-02

  • Stripe's standard rate for a successful online transaction with a domestically issued card is 2.9%.
  • Stripe charges a 30-cent fixed fee per successful online transaction for domestic cards, in addition to its percentage rate.
  • Stripe adds 0.5% to its standard rate for domestic cards that are keyed in manually instead of entered by the customer online.
  • Stripe adds 1.5% to its standard rate for cards issued outside the United States.
  • Stripe adds a further 1% fee on top of its card rate when currency conversion is required for the sale.

Stripe publishes one headline rate — 2.9% + 30¢ per successful transaction for a domestic card — but that headline is a starting point, not a ceiling. Depending on how the card was entered, where it was issued, and which payment method carried the sale, Stripe adds a surcharge on top of it, or, for ACH and Klarna, swaps in a different rate altogether. The table below runs Stripe's own published rates through the arithmetic for six payment methods, at four sale sizes, so the dollar cost is visible instead of buried behind a plus sign.

A separate section further down covers what Stripe charges outside that table entirely: what a dispute costs, and what it costs to move money out faster than Stripe's standard payout schedule.

What the table shows

Six rows, four columns: each row is one Stripe payment method and each column is one sale size, and every cell above was computed the same way so you can read straight across or straight down. The online card row starts from Stripe's plain published rate. The three rows under it keep that same starting rate and layer surcharges on top of it: one for a manually keyed card, one for a card issued abroad, and two at once for a foreign card whose sale also needs currency conversion. The Klarna pay-over-time row runs on its own published rate rather than a surcharge on the card rate. ACH Direct Debit breaks the 'every cell is arithmetic' pattern in one place: Stripe publishes a flat dollar cap on that fee, and once the computed percentage would cross it, the cell shows the cap itself instead of a calculation — that is why the two largest sale sizes in the ACH row read the same figure while the two smallest do not.

Why the headline rate isn't the whole story

2.9% + 30¢ is Stripe's rate for the simplest case: a domestic card, entered online, settled without currency conversion. Move away from any one of those conditions and Stripe layers a surcharge on top, and the surcharges are cumulative — a card that is both foreign-issued and needs currency conversion carries both surcharges at once, not just the larger of the two. None of the three surcharge rows in the table above change the 30-cent fixed fee; only the percentage moves.

Paying with an international card

Two rows above cover what changes when a card was not issued in the United States. Stripe's answer to a foreign card alone is a flat 1.5% on top of its domestic rate; its answer to a foreign card that also needs currency conversion is that same 1.5%, plus another 1% for the conversion itself, both stacked onto the domestic rate at once rather than the larger of the two replacing it. The second row exists for the sale where the money itself has to change currency, not merely for a card that came from another country.

Why ACH favors large payments

ACH Direct Debit runs on a lower headline percentage than any card row in the table above, and it carries no fixed fee at all — but the reason it favors large payments specifically is the cap. Below the amount where 0.8% of the sale would cross Stripe's published dollar cap, the ACH row is ordinary arithmetic, exactly like every other row in the table above. Above that amount, the fee stops growing altogether: the cap becomes the entire cost, no matter how much larger the sale gets from there. The two largest sale sizes in the ACH row read the same dollar figure for exactly that reason, and a business moving a large recurring payment through ACH instead of a card pays that capped figure while every card row above keeps growing with the sale.

Other Stripe fees

The fees below fall outside the payment-method table above because they are not priced per sale — they apply to disputes and to payout timing instead.

Stripe charges a dispute received fee of $15.00 for each dispute a business receives, and a separate dispute countered fee of $15.00 for each dispute the business responds to manually. Moving money out of a Stripe balance faster than the standard payout schedule costs 1.5% of the Instant Payouts volume moved, with a minimum fee of 50¢ per payout.

FAQ

How much does Stripe keep from a $100 sale?

On a $100 sale paid online with a domestic card, Stripe keeps $3.20 — its 2.9% rate plus the 30¢ per-transaction fee.

What does Stripe charge for an international card?

Stripe adds 1.5% to its standard rate for cards issued outside the United States. On a $100 sale paid with an international card, that brings Stripe's cut to $4.70.

Is ACH cheaper than a card on Stripe?

Yes, at every sale size compared here. Stripe charges 0.8% of the transaction for ACH Direct Debit, capped at $5.00, against 2.9% + 30¢ for an online card. On a $100 sale, ACH keeps $0.80 for Stripe versus $3.20 for an online card.

What does a dispute cost on Stripe?

Stripe charges $15.00 for each dispute a business receives, and a separate $15.00 for each dispute the business responds to manually.

Before you rely on these numbers

  • Stripe's own published rates are the only ones checked here — not PayPal's or Square's; that three-way comparison lives on a sibling page.
  • Stripe's fee page states the ACH Direct Debit cap as a flat dollar amount rather than a formula, so the two largest sale sizes in the ACH row use that published cap directly instead of a computed percentage — see 'What the table shows' above for how those two cells differ from the rest of the table.
  • Stripe's fee page also lists rates for in-person card payments, card-verification add-ons, Adaptive Pricing and Link — all left out because none of them maps onto a single row of the table above.
  • Stripe changes its published rates from time to time; every figure here reflects the pricing page on the retrieval date shown next to each source.
